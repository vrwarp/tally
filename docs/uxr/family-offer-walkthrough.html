Tally · design refinement

The kiosk stopped guessing who came with you

A parent types four digits, taps one child, and the kiosk offers the rest of the family — guessed from the phone number the church has on file. Until now those names arrived ticked, and one press of a green button took all of them.

The guess is frequently right about the household and wrong about this morning. It is also built on a roster that carries parents mis-filed as children, imported with two years of attendance from the church's previous check-in kiosk — which means a 41-year-old passes the “came to two of the last three” test better than a child who joined in September, arrives pre-ticked, and gets a sticker and a place in a room count nobody can reconcile.

Worse, the tick fails open: where a gathering has no history yet, or the read fails, or the tablet is cold, every name the guess turned up arrives ticked. On a gathering whose access has been restricted — a nursery, typically — that is every Sunday.

So the offer stays exactly as wide, and nothing arrives ticked but the child who was tapped. The question this refinement had to answer is what that costs, and what stops it being silent.

How it was arrived at

  1. The risk the change creates is precise: a parent's hands do tap-child, press-green, walk. Today the commit button reads the same plain verb whether one child is going or one of four, and it is the only control they are looking at — because their thumb is steering to it.
  2. Three candidates were built into the real component behind default-off props and mounted in the kiosk harness, so every frame is the app rather than a drawing of it: a button that names who is going, a room stated in a masthead above the glass, and a button that counts the chosen against the offered.
  3. Three critics read each round at 800 × 1280 — one asking whether the parent can finish the job on the tablet they are holding, one whether the screen is composed, one what should come off it.
  4. The frames overturned the starting favourite twice. The naming candidate read byte-identically with zero, two and seven children left unticked, which makes it a null treatment against the one failure the round was convened for; and it truncated a 40-character name to an ellipsis on the one control with no undo. A hybrid that named the family only where everybody offered was included survived a round longer and then collapsed, because the case where naming is safe is also the case with two names in it, and two names do not fit.
  5. What ships is a bare count of who is going, on the pickup as well as the arrival — the direction this screen's own header calls the dangerous one, and the one no candidate had been wired to.
  6. The count carries no denominator, and that was the last thing to go. “Check in 1 of 8” was built first and read as a set to be completed: the strongest element on the glass invited a parent to work the number up to eight, and the eight is every child the phone guess turned up — a nursery toddler among them, on a kiosk bound to the children's programme. A guard against leaving somebody out must not become an argument for taking somebody who belongs in another room. What is left is the presence of a number at all, which only happens when other names were offered and are not going.

Try it

The real screens, over a fixture roster and no network. Tap Ramona: her brother and sister are offered and neither is ticked, and the button says how many are going. Tick one and watch the number move. Nothing here is a recording — it is the same components the frames below were shot from.

Two ticks the parent never made become two ticks they did

Before drag to compare After

Before, Noah and Priya arrive ticked because they answer to the same four digits and cleared this gathering's prediction. The button says “Check in all 3”, and a parent who taps their own child and presses green has checked in two more without reading a name. After, nothing is ticked but Ramona — and the button says so, in the one place a thumb is already aimed.

  • Nothing arrives ticked but the child who was tapped, and the tick now fails closed: no participation data means tick that child and nobody else, where it used to mean tick everybody.
  • The commit counts who is going — “Check in 1” — so the label changes the moment somebody is being left behind. Where nobody else was offered it stays the plain verb, so the number's presence is itself the signal. It is the only one of the three candidates whose label moves at all; the naming candidate read identically at zero and at seven.
  • The offer itself is untouched. Every name the guess turned up is still listed at full weight, one tap from being included.
  • The room this check-in is for is named on the screen for the first time, and the grade line pays for it.

02

Seven guessed, at the cap

Parent · the worst case the guess produces

Lobby tablet · 800 × 1280 · portrait

Eight people, one press, and no scroll

Before drag to compare After

This is the frame that decides whether a fail-closed tick is safe to ship, and the frame that settled the button's wording. Before, a press took all eight. After, a hidden row would be a child standing in the building with no record — so the question is whether the list can print the cap without a fold. On this glass it can: seven rows, no “more below”, and the commit does not move.

  • All seven offered names print without scrolling at 800 × 1280, measured rather than asserted — and the room line costs no visible row.
  • The button reads “Check in 1” against seven unticked names. An earlier draft read “1 of 8”, which turned the strongest element on the screen into an invitation to reach eight — over a list that includes children from the other programme.
  • The commit keeps a fixed height and clips rather than wraps. A label that wrapped would take a second line out of the very track the offer list measures itself into, and the list is the one thing here allowed to shrink.

03

One child, nothing guessed

Parent · the commonest journey on this screen

Lobby tablet · 800 × 1280 · portrait

The screen gets quieter, not busier

Before drag to compare After

The refinement adds a room and a longer button, so the case it must not tax is the one most parents are in. With nothing guessed there is nobody to be left behind, so there is nothing for a count to be against — and a caption reading “Anyone else?” was labelling an empty list, one line to dismiss on the way to green.

  • The button stays the plain verb when no family was offered. The count is spent only where somebody could be left behind.
  • The “Anyone else?” caption goes when there is no list under it. The plate below carries its own plus and its own noun.
  • The room replaces the grade rather than standing beside it — a parent told Room 104 does not need the classification the room was derived from.

The pickup

The direction this screen calls the dangerous one

Before drag to compare After

A stray check-in is self-correcting when the child walks in anyway; a stray check-out is a claim that somebody took a child out of the building. None of the three candidates had been wired to it — the round would have closed on arrival frames alone and shipped the pickup unchanged. The frames caught that, and the same guard now carries across.

  • “Check out 1” — the count applies to the pickup as well as the arrival, in the brand ramp this screen already spends on handing children back.
  • One new string rather than three: the count ports to the check-out with a single key in each of the four catalogues, where a naming shape would have needed nine.

The same screen in Spanish

The naming candidate could not survive here: “Registrar a Guadalupe y Maximiliano” is half again the width the button has. A bare count is a fixed short shape in all four catalogues — and the room line, which first said the gathering's name as well, was the one string arriving in English on a Spanish screen.

  • The room is the room alone. The gathering's name is constant for the life of a kiosk's binding, tells nobody where to walk, and was the half that could not be translated.
  • Dropping the denominator took an awkward construction with it: the Spanish count had inherited a personal “a” from the naming string, where it is correct before a name and a preposition looking for a person before a numeral.

Every frame on this page is the real application. The “before” shots were captured from the running app against a seeded forty-five-student ministry; the “after” shots were captured the same way, from the same stack, once the changes were implemented. Nothing here is a mockup, and nothing was re-drawn to make a point.
